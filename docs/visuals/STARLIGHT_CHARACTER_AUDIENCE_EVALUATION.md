# Starlight character audience evaluation

**Status:** research protocol; no candidate is production approved

## The question we are actually testing

The design target is not the “most lovable robot.” It is the form that helps the intended person correctly understand:

1. what the agent specializes in;
2. how it behaves while working;
3. what evidence or artifact it produces;
4. what it cannot do;
5. when a human remains responsible;
6. whether the person would choose to work with it again.

Likeability, anthropomorphism, perceived intelligence, perceived safety, and calibrated reliance are different variables. The Godspeed questionnaire series provides established perception measures for several of them, but Starlight must add role recognition, authority calibration, and adoption intent because a beautiful character can still communicate the wrong operational contract.

Research also warns against assuming that more human features create better collaboration. Large, childlike eyes can increase facial trustworthiness judgments, yet eyes can encourage mental-state attribution; other task studies found that anthropomorphic appearance did not improve calibrated trust and that meaningful uncertainty communication mattered more. Starlight therefore treats warmth as useful only when it preserves operational truth.

## Two evaluation tracks

### Human track

Test appeal, role comprehension, emotional response, trust calibration, memorability, and willingness to adopt with people from the actual target audience.

### Agent and system track

Software agents do not have a human aesthetic preference to measure. Test whether the visual package is machine-operable instead:

- stable asset IDs and agent IDs;
- exact role, skills, state, provenance, permissions, and confidence in structured data;
- deterministic alt text and captions;
- accessible color-independent state encoding;
- consistent crops and embeddings;
- graph and workflow links that do not depend on visual inference;
- no generated text used as a control, claim, or status label.

An image model or critic may screen artifacts for defects, resemblance, and rubric coverage. It cannot replace target-user research or human brand approval.

## Study design

### Stage 0 — quality and rights screen

Reject obvious anatomy errors, broken mechanics, fake text, accidental brand imitation, unsafe stereotypes, and missing provenance before exposing candidates to participants. Keep rejected work in the evidence ledger.

### Stage 1 — five-second role test

Show one candidate without its name or caption for five seconds. Ask participants to choose its likely specialty and working behavior from a randomized set. Record first choice, confidence, and time. This measures whether function survives before lore and polish.

### Stage 2 — task-context test

Show the same candidate inside four exact contexts:

- profile hero;
- 96-pixel directory or graph node;
- governed workflow with evidence and a human gate;
- Academy lesson or onboarding scene.

Ask what the agent is doing, what it can change, and who can approve the consequential outcome. This catches authority errors that an isolated portrait hides.

### Stage 3 — perception and adoption measures

Use a compact semantic-differential instrument derived from the validated Godspeed constructs, then add Starlight-specific measures:

- likeability;
- perceived competence;
- perceived safety;
- anthropomorphism and animacy;
- role clarity;
- confidence calibration;
- authority-boundary clarity;
- distinctiveness from familiar entertainment or consumer robots;
- willingness to try, install, keep, and recommend the agent pack;
- comfort returning after the agent says “I do not know” or asks for human help.

Do not merge all measures into one “appeal” number. A high-stakes guardian should not lose merely because an Academy companion is cuter.

### Stage 4 — delayed recall

After ten minutes and several unrelated tasks, ask participants to identify the agent, specialty, and boundary again. Memorability without correct role recall is visual noise.

### Stage 5 — pairwise choice by surface

Run randomized pairwise comparisons within one surface and consequence level. Never compare a chibi Academy guide with a governance guardian as if they were substitutes.

## Audience structure

The first moderated pilot should include 24–36 people across founders/operators, technical builders, creators/educators, researchers/public-interest practitioners, and less-technical prospective users. It is directional research, not a population estimate.

Before adopting a global canonical default, run an unmoderated randomized study with a sample size justified by a power analysis. Segment results by role, technical fluency, age band, culture/region, accessibility needs, prior AI-agent use, and familiarity with games or animation. Do not optimize exclusively for the founder cohort.

Academy experiences intended for children or vulnerable users require a separate ethical and accessibility review. Do not infer suitability from adult “cute” ratings.

## Decision model

Score each candidate per surface on six equally visible dimensions:

| Dimension | Evidence |
| --- | --- |
| Role recognition | specialty and artifact identified correctly in five seconds |
| Behavioral truth | temperament inferred from work behavior rather than face alone |
| Trust calibration | reliance and permission judgments match the displayed contract |
| Distinctiveness | remembered with correct role and low franchise confusion |
| Adoption intent | appropriate willingness to try, keep, and recommend |
| Production fitness | crop, accessibility, performance, provenance, and maintainability pass |

The art team may recommend a synthesis instead of a winner. Human approval records the selected morphology, eye grammar, rendering lane, surfaces, and rejected alternatives. The result is a governed visual contract, not a timeless claim that one face is universally best.
